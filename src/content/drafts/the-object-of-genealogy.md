Criticism of Foucault's work often traces its shared argumentative lineage to
the commentaries of Nancy Fraser, Jürgen Habermas, and Charles Taylor. Appearing
after the publication and popularization of *Discipline and Punish*, these
commentaries generally raise issues with Foucault's work by claiming that he
does not provide enough reassurance, nor have the conceptual resources to argue,
that the dominations he depicts are able to be transformed and thus escaped.
Fraser and Taylor claim that he does not have a sufficient normative position
from which to articulate his criticism (Fraser 1981; Taylor 1984). Habermas
suggests more strongly that Foucault's work not only relies on a normative
framework, but one which is specifically hidden in order to make its criticisms
appear radical. While Taylor in particular relaxed his claims after the later
publication of Foucault's lectures complicated the concepts developed in his
monographs, much of the ensuing commentary has structured itself in relation to
the questions these original interlocutors raised. Thus, in hoping to develop a
novel understanding of Foucault's genealogical project, it is necessary to
divorce contemporary commentaries from their framing in response to normative
critiques. Instead, I will attempt to understand what generalized
characterization of genealogy these commentaries can offer.

In service of such a goal, I will specifically seek to understand genealogy as
an abstract method of knowledge production about a corresponding abstract
object, i.e. a set of structural practices for producing knowledge about an
object which possesses certain qualities. This strategy of interpretation is
chosen, indeed, in accordance with Foucault (Foucault 2003, 6–10; Foucault 2008,
35–37), but also because it services one's understanding of genealogy as a
methodology for critical scholarship. Because scholarship takes the structure of
generalized knowledge production, it would be necessary for genealogy as a
methodology within this structure to correspond to and support it. Thus, in
examining commentary on Foucault's work, instead of reading it in response to
normative critique, I hope to understand it as developing genealogy as an
abstract methodology of knowledge production.

Commentators have identified many aspects of Foucault's genealogical work, but I
will be focusing on the two most prominent. In the first place, commentators
like Martin Saar and Raymond Geuss have noted that Foucault's investigations
primarily operate by means of subverting or destabilizing established concepts
(Saar 2002, 2008; Geuss 2002). From this perspective, genealogy works by
presenting historiography which unmasks the supposed neutrality and universality
of its targeted concepts. *Discipline and Punish*, following this example, would
be understood to function not only by developing an alternative historiography
of the modern prison system, but specifically by deploying this history to
contest the received notion that this system is morally superior to the previous
because it targets what 'truly' and 'eternally' underlies crime. Other
commentators, most notably Amy Allen and Colin Koopman, suggest that Foucault
works less to overtly critique practices through their historicization but
rather to do so in order to open them up to continual critique (Allen 2018;
Koopman 2013). In particular, they go as far as to say that this kind of
critical attitude (a notion which they mobilize from Foucault's later work)
forms the basis for freedom or critique as such (Allen 2015, 2016).

The former interpretation suggests that genealogy is directed towards concrete
objects of a twofold character. It particularly treats objects which
simultaneously "frame and shape human action and self-understanding" (Saar 2008,
302) and "had no significant history before \[the genealogy\]" (Saar 2002, 233).
Particularly, for Saar, genealogies focus on those objects for which this
ahistoricity itself contributes to the foundational role that the object plays
in the subject's self-understanding. Genealogies, he thus suggests, are not mere
historicizations of any objects whatsoever, but address objects in relation to
the role they play in making the subject up, as well as this subject itself
(Saar 2008, 301–302). This twofold concrete object, however, corresponds to a
singular abstract object. The abstract object of which genealogy produces
knowledge in this framing is thus the relationship between these two concrete
objects, the making-up of the subject and the (a)historicization process itself.
This latter indeterminacy in particular characterizes the abstract object of
interest, as Saar notes that genealogy "goes beyond the mere historicization of
its object in that it relates historical processes to the eﬀects they have on
the construction and self-constitution of human actors" (Saar 2008, 301). It is
thus not merely the act of historicization which constitutes the specificity of
genealogy, but the relationship between this making-historical and the
making-self which it identifies and treats as its abstract object. Genealogy is
unlike other procedures of knowledge production, for Saar, because of the
destabilizing effect, to be sure, but specifically this effect as is produced by
knowledge about the relationship between historicity and subjectivication. One
could understand this as a disruption of necessity conditioned by
knowledge-level ahistoricity through the introduction of the knowledge of
historicity. This latter point bears repeating: genealogy, following Saar, does
not produce historicity itself, but rather knowledge of it. Genealogy attempts
to disrupt subjectivation by construing it as something which relies upon
knowledge, then producing knowledge so as to adjust the results of this process.
Thus, when genealogy targets historicity, it can only do by producing knowledge
about it; but knowledge of an object does not constitute that object itself. In
understanding genealogy as having a particular abstract object, the distinction
must thus be maintained between this object—in this instance the relationship
between historicity and subjectivation—and the object itself. In turning to
further commentaries, one finds a similar structure.

The latter commentators, though in nominal disagreement with Saar, refine the
terms of the abstract object which he describes. Allen and Koopman in particular
provide insights as to what distinguishes the character of the concrete
historicity towards which genealogy directs its attention. Allen suggests that
genealogical work directs itself towards "lines of fragility and fracture,"
(Allen 2015, 524), providing, in Koopman's terms, a "description of the details
of our contingency" (Koopman 2013, 144). While Koopman and Allen develop these
framings of genealogy to suggest a structure of 'problematizing' which differs
from Saar's 'denaturalizing,' understood as an abstract procedure of knowledge
production, genealogy on their terms addresses itself towards an interrelated
abstract object. In Allen's framing, the kind of historical inquiry in which
genealogy is engaged is particularly concerned with the demonstration of
contingency, and thus, making-problematic (Allen 2016, 190–95). Contingency as
such forms the abstract object of this understanding of genealogy, insofar as it
is tied up in historicity and the making-historical. Contingency is contained
in, and implied by, historicity (Allen 2016, 192), so one may understand this as
a refinement of Saar's abstract object. Genealogy thus directs itself towards
producing knowledge of historicity insofar as this concrete historicity is
supposed to be the foundation for making up an abstract subject, and in order to
do so it demonstrates the contingency of which this historicity is made.

This operative notion of contingency, on which these prominent interpretations
depend (Allen 2018, 175–6; Koopman 2016, 99), is related to historicity insofar
as it is the content of which historicity is made up. Because contingency
implies the negation of its opposite, necessity, genealogical investigation must
reject understanding itself as a project of determinate negation (Koopman 2016,
99). Instead, genealogy embraces contingency and thus possibility as the content
of historicity itself, treating these three notions as interrelated, concrete
determinations which form the abstract object about which it produces knowledge.
It is thus that one understands the presence of language of 'opening up,'
'making possible,' etc., which these commentators frequently deploy, because per
their interpretation genealogical work renders historicity, therefore
contingency, therefore possibility, known. However, here one encounters an
impasse.

If genealogy is an abstract procedure for producing knowledge about historicity
and contingency, how can it be understood that genealogy is itself, in the words
of a further interpreter, "possibilizing" (Lorenzini 2020)? The strongest
version of this claim would suggest that by producing knowledge of this
historical possibility, subjects will make use of this knowledge and change
these conditions based on an understanding of their contingency (Saar 2002). But
the particular jump being made here, that knowledge itself produces alterity, is
one of necessity, not possibility. It would need to not only be possible, but
necessary, that by producing such knowledge, alterity always already comes into
being. In fact, because genealogy suggests that contingency, and thus
possibility, are the very constituents of historicity themselves, genealogy
produces knowledge about instances of abstract objects which it depends on
already being extant. Genealogy thus supposes that it produces knowledge about
possibility and contingency which is itself already the content of history;
nothing additional, i.e. truly other, comes into being. If I insisted on the
distinction between knowledge and its object earlier, it is because at this
foundational level of the genealogical method such a distinction appears
confused. Genealogy would need, if it is to intervene in the process of
subjectivation, to produce actual possibility, to adjust the conditions which
make up subjects by the knowledge which results from it.

In this sense, the common interpretation of genealogy can be understood as a
form of reinscribing its own conditions of existence. Because genealogical work
is insistent that it makes historicized possibility and contingency actual but
what it truly makes actual is knowledge of these things, it functions as a kind
of ideological barricade; critical energy is directed towards knowledge
production as an end in itself in the belief that knowledge will reproduce the
object of which it is, stopping any further production of that object. If one
wanted to produce possibility, to make it actual, it would be a reduction of
possibility to something other than itself, something which is not knowledge but
which is instead an object about which knowledge could be produced. Genealogy
cannot produce this object, and because of its insistence that it can, it
prevents exactly the production which it seeks.

Genealogy, when thus seen as an abstract form of knowledge production about an
abstract object which is itself the content of historicity—contingency qua
possibility—insists that knowledge produces alterity; yet, because of the
composition of genealogical knowledge-production, this is structurally
foreclosed. What results from genealogy is knowledge of possibility and
alterity, not these things themselves. Because genealogical work is insistent,
however, that through it these things come into being, while at the same time
only producing knowledge of them, it prevents exactly what it sets out to do.
Genealogy thus functions as an ideological reproduction of its structural
conditions of possibility.
